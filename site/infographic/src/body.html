When reactors share analyzers, which reactor and which moment does a result belong to?

A process-development lab runs many small bioreactors but only a couple of at-line analyzers. Every sample travels from a vessel to an instrument, and the problem is the context a bare number can lose on the way: which reactor, which moment, which instrument state.

capacity

The capacity paradox: a light average load, and still a queue?

An all-in-one at-line analyzer is fast, and two of them keep the average load light. The question is whether samples can still wait.

Derived ceiling: 60 min ÷ 4.5 min is about 13.3 samples per hour per analyzer, before QC, calibration and maintenance.

The default lab asks for 24 reactors × 1.4 to 1.6 samples per reactor-day, which is 33.6 to 38.4 samples a day. At 4.5 min each that is 151 to 173 min of analyzer time. The sample counts and the window are assumptions.

  1. Draw at 08:40. sampled_at is the instant the sample leaves the process.
  2. Bolus feed at 09:00. The reactor's state changes while the sample waits.
  3. Measurement at 09:25. measured_at is the instant the run ends.
  4. Two joins. One keeps the draw time and sees the state the sample came from; the other keeps the measurement time and may not.

hold_time

run_started_at minus sampled_at, truth stamps. It ends when the run starts, so it is the 45 min span minus the 4.5 min run: 40.5 min. The queue wait is only part of it.

turnaround

available_at minus sampled_at. It includes measurement, ingestion and review, and tells whether a result could inform a decision.

Why two clocks

With one stored time (the measured time) the draw time cannot be reconstructed, and neither rule above can run.

What held time does to the sample

In the cited study, CHO samples held neat for up to 55 min at room temperature showed no decrease in viability according to the authors; they attribute the decline they measured to the diluent. The reported neat slope of −0.03 percentage points per minute carries no significance test and is valid to 55 min. The simulator applies a slope of 0 to neat samples, and a diluent or handling slope (down to −7.2 percentage points per hour) is a scenario knob.

The 30 min hold limit used elsewhere in the design is a blood-gas rule borrowed from another domain and applied, as an assumption, to every analyte.

Second case: an offline pH that re-standardises the online probe

A common practice treats the offline pH value as the source of truth and readjusts the online probe onto it when the two differ by more than a set criterion. Sampling procedure, equipment, hold time, temperature shift and CO2 degassing all add offsets to the offline value, and at higher cell density pH should not be considered stable after sampling. An offset that is wrong in the record can therefore propagate into the controller.

architectures

One sample, three ways to record it

Three hard-coded architectures would hide which mechanism makes the difference. The design uses ten independent feature flags instead. A, B and C are presets of those flags (ADR-0005, ADR-0017, ADR-0020), and the ablation is C with exactly one flag switched off. B is built from features that simple laboratory systems really have: barcodes, automatic capture and an audit trail.

lineage

Lineage is a graph, and the hard part is the merge

Each step from a reactor to a result is an edge. Following the edges backwards from any result finds its origin batches. One batch gives a tree; a pool built from several batches makes the relation many-to-many.

  1. Draw. The sample keeps its unit and batch keys from the moment of writing; they are never re-keyed afterwards.
  2. Aliquot. Portions of one sample go to different analyses; their volumes sum to at most the sample.
  3. Run. Each pass through an analyzer is a run that links to an instrument state. A failed QC holds the results and requests a rerun.
  4. Results. A correction is a new version that supersedes the old one. A query as of time t sees the latest version available by t.
  5. Pool. Merging material from two batches makes the lineage many-to-many; walking the edges backwards from the sample finds both origin batches.

One harvest split over several cycles and re-pooled still has a single origin batch. Many-to-many appears only when batches merge.

systems

Where the record lives: system boundaries and integration reality

Several systems each hold part of the story: the historian holds process tags such as U03.pH, the batch record holds events, and some system holds the in-process test result. Which one owns routine in-process results is site-dependent, so the model treats it as a setting.

Vendor material disagrees on which system should own in-process checks, which is why the design leaves it as a setting.

Getting results out of an instrument

CSV file drop (baseline)

The all-in-one analyzer exports a CSV file to a shared folder on its bridge computer. The simulator models this path first. The file carries a local time, often without a stated zone.

Vendor OPC server

A vendor OPC server exists as a separately licensed add-on. The simulator does not model it by default.

SiLA 2, OPC UA LADS (future)

One vendor map describes SiLA 2 adoption as slow and OPC UA LADS as early on installed fleets.

Clocks and time zones are a data-integrity control

UK MHRA guidance asks for access to controlled or synchronised clocks for recording timed events and for specifying the time zone (section 5.1). The WHO guidance lists restricting the ability to change any clock used for recording timed events, including clocks in process instrumentation (section 4.12). The design stores every instant in UTC with the source's own offset beside it.

How often a clock is wrong has no source. The wrong-clock scenario (SC6) uses a +5 h offset on one analyzer as a neutral stress case.

EU Annex 11

The European Commission lists Annex 11 on computerised systems as the January 2011 revision. That is the text this page treats as current.

A revised Annex 11, a revised Chapter 4 and a new draft Annex 22 on AI went to consultation from 7 July to 7 October 2025. The revision is a draft and not final.

The model describes controls. It does not claim compliance with either text.

method

Method: five layers, one observation contract

The engine runs a hidden truth world and an observable recorded world side by side. Recorders see only what a person or an instrument could see, and the evaluator alone compares the two.

  • Recorders consume observable events only. A test changes hidden-only fields and requires the recorder output to stay byte-identical.
  • The same scenario, seed and engine version give a byte-identical event log. Randomness is keyed by sample, analyte, attempt and purpose, so adding a sample never changes another sample's draws.
  • Stochastic comparisons run over at least 20 seeds and are reported as a spread across seeds, without confidence intervals. The minimum is a threshold of the design and proves nothing about statistical sufficiency. The comparisons are never gated, and no test requires that one architecture wins.
  • Online sensor series are recorded at a 60 s cadence in the truth world.

  • Every figure on this page carries a ledger id. A repository check follows each assumption id to its sources.
  • A source is verified at one of three levels: seen as an excerpt, read by the research executor, or re-read by the orchestrator. This page cites only the re-read sources without a label. Anything else is marked provisional.
  • The engine gate is structural: hand-calculated fixtures, a queueing check against analytic waiting times, and a forced-error fixture for every flag. Orderings between architectures are reported, never asserted.

The plant in the lab is a scene you can walk through. The clock plays, pauses, speeds up and moves to any instant of the run. Each data architecture has a record hall whose shelves hold a card for each sample that record keeps. On request a view shows what the simulation knows and no record can hold, marked as the simulation's own view. Operator actions, such as adding a feed or taking an analyzer down, rerun the plant, and a card or a reactor links to the Lineage and Results screens. A short guided scene follows one sample that was drawn before a feed and measured after it. The Break-out cases in the plant exist in the local build only.

The lab's Break-out screen and the Break-out cases in the plant are held back in the public build until the sources behind them are re-read. In that screen a hold is a state of the record and stops no culture. Its containment figures show the effect of tracking and holding on the record, and its confirming tests are judged by coverage, not by a sensitivity. Per-reactor cell-line profiles and operating-mode changes such as batch, fed-batch and perfusion (S4) are designed but not built yet.
